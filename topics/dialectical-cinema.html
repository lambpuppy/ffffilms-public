Dialectical cinema

24 names across 5 concepts

In 1983, Harun Farocki filmed Jean-Marie Straub and Danièle Huillet preparing their adaptation of Kafka's Amerika. A year later he acted in the finished film, Class Relations, as Delamarche. The two careers touch in that gesture, and the standard accounts often file them apart incorrectly: Farocki under the essay film, Straub and Huillet under the political cinema of the 1960s. Their work belongs to one argument, carried on for fifty years and across two countries, about what cinema is for once it stops being an instrument of illusion.

The argument is usually called Brechtian, and sometimes materialist or dialectical. Its premise is easy to state and hard to practise. A film should not let the viewer disappear into a story. The text is set against the image so that the two can be compared, the sound is recorded where the shot is made, and the camera is planted at the exact place where something happened. The shot is counted, and the spectator is left with the work of putting image, sound, and text together. Politics, in this tradition, is carried by the form.

The tradition has a second inheritance, from the French critique of the spectacle. Guy Debord defined the spectacle as a social relation between people, mediated by images, rather than a collection of images. Jean Baudrillard went further, claiming that the image no longer copies a reality, and instead produces one that never had an original. Both claims are too large to test, and both were answered in practice by filmmakers who inspect images at close range. What follows traces the movement's lineage, the concepts it argued over, and the names that carried it.

Historical & theoretical lineage

Brecht and epic theatre

Bertolt Brecht asked the theatre to stop letting the audience identify with the characters, so that the machinery of the story would show. His epic theatre set narration against scene and kept the spectator awake to the construction. Farocki named the critical theatre of Brecht and Heiner Müller, and the documentary theatre of Peter Weiss, among the things that formed him.

Pioneers

  • Bertolt Brecht → A Short Organum for the Theatre (1948) · The Business Affairs of Mr Julius Caesar (1937–39)
  • Heiner Müller → Hamletmachine
  • Peter Weiss

The critique of the spectacle and the simulation

Guy Debord's The Society of the Spectacle (1967) argued that images had become the medium of social relations, and that this belonged to the commodity. He filmed the book in 1973, setting quoted images and white text against a black screen. Jean Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation (1981) removed the original altogether: the image, he wrote, produces a real without a model. Debord asked for refusal, and Baudrillard described a condition in which refusal has nowhere to stand. The filmmakers of this line answer both by inspecting images at close range.

Key thinkers

  • Guy Debord → The Society of the Spectacle (1973) → The Society of the Spectacle (1967)
  • Jean Baudrillard → Simulacra and Simulation (1981)

The two avant-gardes

Peter Wollen's essay The Two Avant-Gardes (1975) separated the formalist avant-garde from a second, political one, and named Godard, Jancsó, Oshima, and Straub-Huillet as its members. Criticism later grouped the same constellation under the name political modernism. D. N. Rodowick's history of the debate asked how the alliance of modernist form and radical politics had held up in film theory.

Key thinkers

  • Peter Wollen → The Two Avant-Gardes (1975)
  • D. N. Rodowick → The Crisis of Political Modernism (1988)

Concepts and the names that extend them

The word against the image (1954–1968)

The line begins with Jean-Marie Straub & Danièle Huillet. Straub moved to Paris in the 1950s and worked as an assistant to Robert Bresson on A Man Escaped (1956) and to Jacques Rivette the same year. What he took from Bresson was a way of filming that refused decoration: the camera at the place where the event happened, the sound recorded there, the gesture left without emphasis. With Huillet he made Machorka-Muff (1963), from a Heinrich Böll story about a former officer in the new Federal Republic, and Not Reconciled (1965) from Böll's Billiards at Half-Past Nine, which opens with Brecht's instructions to actors and carries the sentence Only Violence Helps Where Violence Reigns as a title. Bertolt Brecht is the shared reference: his epic theatre had asked the audience to stop identifying and start judging, and these filmmakers read him as a method instead of a style.

In West Germany, Alexander Kluge signed the Oberhausen Manifesto in 1962 and began a practice between cinema, television, and writing. Harun Farocki entered the DFFB in 1966 and made his first film the next year. In France, Guy Debord published The Society of the Spectacle in 1967, and Jean-Luc Godard and Jean-Pierre Gorin began making films together in 1968, forming the Dziga Vertov Group around 1969. The counter-cinema had its theory and its first films at the same moment.

The image put to work (1968–1984)

The Dziga Vertov Group made four films between 1969 and 1971, among them Wind from the East (1970) and Vladimir and Rosa (1971), and dissolved. Harun Farocki made Inextinguishable Fire in 1969, with Helke Sander as assistant; rather than show the burns he was describing, he stubbed a cigarette out on his own forearm and named Dow Chemical as the maker of napalm. Peter Nestler had been filming mines, rivers, and roads for television since the early 1960s, and in 1972 he read the Brecht passage in Straub-Huillet's Introduction to Arnold Schoenberg's Accompaniment to a Cinematographic Scene. Farocki became an editor of Filmkritik in 1974, and the magazine devoted two issues to Nestler's films; Hartmut Bitomsky, his DFFB colleague, wrote for the same pages and made his own films from the images of German industry.

Straub and Huillet worked from Brecht's novel fragment in History Lessons (1972), from Schönberg's opera in Moses and Aaron (1975), and from Franco Fortini's book in Fortini/Cani (1976). Class Relations (1984) came from Kafka's Amerika, with Farocki playing Delamarche and, the year before, filming them at work. Kluge contributed three segments to the collective Germany in Autumn (1978), and Debord filmed The Society of the Spectacle in 1973. In 1975 Peter Wollen's essay The Two Avant-Gardes separated this political avant-garde from the formalist one and named Miklós Jancsó and Nagisa Ōshima beside Godard and Straub-Huillet.

A method in exile (1984–2000)

After Class Relations the Straubs kept to the texts: Hölderlin's The Death of Empedocles (1987), Brecht's adaptation of Sophocles' Antigone (1992), and Vittorini's Conversations in Sicily as Sicilia! (1999). Danièle Huillet died in 2006; Straub kept working until his death in 2022. Farocki moved from television essays to the gallery: Images of the World and the Inscription of War (1988), How to Live in the FRG (1990), Workers Leaving the Factory (1995), and installations from 1996 in which two images stand side by side so that neither replaces the other. Bitomsky turned to the motorway and the car in Reichsautobahn (1986) and Der VW-Komplex (1989).

Pedro Costa filmed the Straubs editing Sicilia! for Where Does Your Hidden Smile Lie? (2001) and carried their materialism to Lisbon's Fontainhas district in In Vanda's Room (2000). Jean-Claude Rousseau made La Vallée close (1995), backed by the Straubs, alone with a small camera. John Gianvito's Profit Motive and the Whispering Wind (2007) crossed the United States in long fixed shots of the memorials of its labour history.

The line today (2000 onwards)

The Straubs' late films kept the method intact: Workers, Peasants (2001) from Vittorini and These Encounters of Theirs (2006). Pedro Costa went further into digital video with Colossal Youth (2006) and Vitalina Varela (2019). Tariq Teguia's Rome Rather Than You (2006) and Inland (2008) filmed the edges of Algeria in long takes and direct sound. Eloy Enciso filmed Arraianos (2012) with villagers from the Galician border country around a 1960s play, and Longa noite (2019) from letters and testimony of the Franco years. Cyril Schäublin's Unrest (2022) set the young Kropotkin among the watchmakers of Saint-Imier, played by non-professionals and by working watchmakers.

Farocki died in 2014, and the Harun Farocki Institut was founded in Berlin the next year. In 2014, Trevor Paglen's essay Operational Images took up Farocki's term for pictures that machines make for machines, now the ordinary infrastructure of drones, platforms, and data centres. The Brechtian question, who the image is for and who is left outside the frame, has not gone away; it has only become harder to see, because the images are no longer made to be watched by anyone.

Structural characteristics

  • Text set against image. The text is carried as a separate layer, alongside the image: Brecht's novel fragment in History Lessons, Fortini's book in Fortini/Cani, Mao's Little Red Book in Farocki's first film. It is not a script. It is material set against the image.
  • Direct sound, recorded in the take. Nothing is dubbed afterwards. Wind, traffic, and cicadas stay in the mix, and they are subjects beside the voice.
  • The camera planted, the shot counted. Frontal compositions and long takes. The cut is a deliberate step, and duration does the arguing.
  • Work as the subject. Factories, mines, watchmaking, and the labour of filming itself; the conditions of the people in front of the camera are the material.
  • The spectator left with the work. No psychological identification. The viewer assembles the argument from the parts, as Brecht asked of the theatre.

Key artists and thinkers

  1. Bertolt Brecht A Short Organum for the Theatre

    Germany, 1898–1956

    Playwright and director whose epic theatre set narration against scene, kept the audience from disappearing into the characters, and left each scene to be judged. The filmmakers of this line took from it a method rather than a style.

  2. Heiner Müller Hamletmachine

    Germany, 1929–1995

    Playwright of the German Democratic Republic whose texts cut history into fragments and refuse the comfort of a whole story. Farocki named Müller's critical theatre, with Brecht's, among the things that formed him.

  3. Peter Weiss Farocki's naming, Senses of Cinema

    Germany and Sweden, 1916–1982

    Writer, painter, and filmmaker, born near Berlin and based in Sweden from 1939, whose documentary theatre and novel The Aesthetics of Resistance examined fascism and the labour movement. Farocki named his documentary theatre as an influence.

  4. Jean-Marie Straub & Danièle Huillet Letterboxd

    France, 1933–2022 and 1936–2006

    Straub and Huillet made films from texts, recorded sound where the shot was made, and planted the camera in the place itself. Straub assisted Robert Bresson in the 1950s; their films together run from Machorka-Muff (1963) to These Encounters of Theirs (2006).

  5. Jean-Claude Rousseau Letterboxd

    France

    Director who works alone with a small camera and long fixed takes. La Vallée close (1995) was backed by the Straubs, and he shot their ciné-tract Europa 2005 – 27 octobre (2006).

  6. Peter Wollen The Two Avant-Gardes

    UK, 1938–2019

    Film theorist and filmmaker. The Two Avant-Gardes (1975) separated the formalist avant-garde from the political one and named Godard, Jancsó, Ōshima, and Straub-Huillet as the second group's members.

  7. D. N. Rodowick The Crisis of Political Modernism, ch. 1

    US, b. 1952

    Film theorist whose The Crisis of Political Modernism traced the alliance of modernist form and radical politics through the film theory of the 1970s and asked what had held it together.

  8. Miklós Jancsó Letterboxd

    Hungary, 1921–2014

    Hungarian director who staged power and coercion as choreographed movement in long takes: The Round-Up (1965), The Red and the White (1967), Red Psalm (1971). Wollen placed him in the political avant-garde with Godard, Ōshima, and Straub-Huillet.

  9. Nagisa Ōshima Letterboxd

    Japan, 1932–2013

    Japanese director of Cruel Story of Youth (1960), Death by Hanging (1968), and In the Realm of the Senses (1976), who worked through genre and scandal to argue about postwar Japan. Wollen placed him in the second avant-garde.

  10. Alexander Kluge Letterboxd

    Germany, 1932–2026

    Signatory of the Oberhausen Manifesto in 1962 and a central figure of the New German Cinema, working across features, television, and writing. His montages set documents, fiction, and theory beside one another without dissolving the seams.

  11. Guy Debord Letterboxd The Society of the Spectacle

    France, 1931–1994

    Founder of the Situationist International and author of The Society of the Spectacle (1967), which defined the spectacle as a social relation mediated by images. He filmed the book in 1973.

  12. Jean Baudrillard Simulacra and Simulation (extract)

    France, 1929–2007

    Sociologist and theorist whose Simulacra and Simulation (1981) argued that the image had dispensed with the original it once copied.

  13. Jean-Luc Godard Letterboxd

    France, 1930–2022

    Director who formed the Dziga Vertov Group with Jean-Pierre Gorin around 1969 and made four films under the collective name, among them Wind from the East (1970) and Vladimir and Rosa (1971).

  14. Jean-Pierre Gorin Letterboxd

    France, b. 1943

    Co-founder of the Dziga Vertov Group with Godard; after it dissolved he moved to California and made Poto and Cabengo (1979), Routine Pleasures (1986), and My Crasy Life (1992).

  15. Harun Farocki Letterboxd

    Germany, 1944–2014

    Filmmaker and media artist who filmed the production of images themselves: napalm manufacture, surveillance, training simulations, and the factory gate. He edited Filmkritik, taught at the DFFB, and called the pictures made by machines for machines operational images.

  16. Peter Nestler Letterboxd

    Germany, b. 1937

    Documentary filmmaker whose short films on mines, rivers, and steelworks, most of them made for television, let the images make the case. He reads the Brecht passage in Straub-Huillet's Schoenberg film (1972), and Filmkritik devoted two issues to his work under Farocki's editorship.

  17. Hartmut Bitomsky Letterboxd

    Germany, 1942–2025

    Filmmaker of the DFFB generation and a Filmkritik author, whose Deutschlandbilder (1983), Reichsautobahn (1986), and Der VW-Komplex (1989) examine the images of German industry and landscape. Farocki acted in his first short, 3000 Häuser (1967), and advised on Auf Biegen und Brechen (1985).

  18. Helke Sander Letterboxd

    Germany, b. 1937

    Filmmaker and assistant on Inextinguishable Fire (1969). She founded the journal Frauen und Film in 1974 and directed The All-Around Reduced Personality (1978).

  19. Cyril Schäublin Letterboxd

    Switzerland, b. 1984

    Swiss director. Unrest (2022) sets the young Kropotkin among the watchmakers of Saint-Imier in the 1870s, played by non-professionals and by working watchmakers.

  20. Trevor Paglen Letterboxd

    US, b. 1974

    Artist and geographer whose essay Operational Images (2014) took up Farocki's term for the pictures that machines make for machines, and whose work tracks the infrastructure of surveillance and space.

  21. Pedro Costa Letterboxd

    Portugal

    Director whose Fontainhas films with Cape Verdean immigrants, among them In Vanda's Room (2000) and Vitalina Varela (2019), extend the Straubs' materialism into digital video. Where Does Your Hidden Smile Lie? (2001) filmed the Straubs editing Sicilia!.

  22. Eloy Enciso Letterboxd

    Spain, b. 1975

    Galician director. Arraianos (2012) is played by villagers around a 1960s play by Jenaro Marinhas del Valle, and Longa noite (2019) reads letters and testimony from the Franco years.

  23. John Gianvito Letterboxd

    US

    Filmmaker and curator. Profit Motive and the Whispering Wind (2007) crosses the United States in long fixed shots of the graves and memorials of its labour history.

  24. Tariq Teguia Letterboxd

    Algeria, b. 1966

    Director who studied visual arts and philosophy in Paris and films the edges of Algeria. Rome Rather Than You (2006) and Inland (2008) work in long takes and direct sound.
