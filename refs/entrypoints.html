84 filmmakers · 5 clusters · marks read live from the collection

Each entry gives the filmmaker's full name, what the work does, and two starting films. Where the collection already holds something by a filmmaker, the picks are next steps instead of introductions.

(seen) = already watched · (list) = on the watchlist

Transcendental

  1. Nathaniel Dorsky

    USA, b. 1943

    His films are silent and projected at 18 frames per second. They are built on what he calls polyvalent montage, in which shots rhyme with each other instead of leading anywhere. He named and practised a "devotional cinema", and the films work closer to liturgy than to narrative.

    → Compline (2009) · Song and Solitude (2006)

  2. Jerome Hiler

    USA, b. 1943

    He works in colour on 16mm, editing in the camera and layering dense superimpositions, and for forty years he showed almost nothing in public. He is also a scholar of medieval stained glass. The films share its concern with light passing through a coloured surface as a devotional act.

    → Words of Mercury (2011) · New Shores (1971–2016)

  3. Stan Brakhage

    USA, 1933–2003

    His films were hand-painted, scratched, baked, and rapidly cut, and almost always silent. They try to record seeing before language organises it. He made nearly 400 films, and many of the filmmakers in this section work in the space he opened.

    → Anticipation of the Night (1958) · The Act of Seeing with One's Own Eyes (1971) (seen)

  4. Maya Deren

    Ukraine/USA, 1917–1961

    She filmed ritual, dance, and dream logic with a choreographer's control of space and a trance-like editing rhythm. She was also a theorist and an ethnographer of Haitian vodou, and she did much to build the institutions of the American avant-garde.

    → Divine Horsemen (1947–51/1985) · A Study in Choreography for Camera (1945)

  5. Jonas Mekas

    Lithuania/USA, 1922–2019

    He shaped the diary film: bursts of single frames, hand-held glimpses, and voice-over recollections recorded decades after the images. Exile and joy sit in the same gesture, and the home movie becomes a serious form.

    → Lost Lost Lost (1976) · As I Was Moving Ahead… (2000) (seen)

  6. Robert Bresson

    France, 1901–1999

    He built a whole grammar out of refusal. His non-professional "models" are drained of performance, bodies are broken into hands, feet, and door latches, and the soundtrack carries the story that the image holds back. Grace and imprisonment are his two subjects, and most austere filmmakers since have worked with the vocabulary he made.

    → Lancelot du Lac (1974) (seen) · Une femme douce (1969) (seen)

  7. Bruno Dumont

    France, b. 1958

    He casts non-actors from the Nord and places them in wide, flat northern landscapes where a near-catatonic calm breaks without warning into violence or sex. He treats transcendence and idiocy as the same substance, which is why his later turn to grotesque comedy was less of a break than it looked.

    → The Life of Jesus (1997) (list) · Hors Satan (2011)

  8. Maurice Pialat

    France, 1925–2003

    His scenes start late and are cut off before they resolve, so every cut feels like an interruption instead of a shape. He provoked his actors on set to get performances with real hostility in them. It is the opposite of Bresson's control and arrives at the same refusal of dramatic comfort.

    → Loulou (1980) (list) · Van Gogh (1991)

  9. Pedro Costa

    Portugal, b. 1959

    He works on digital video with an immobile camera and a chiaroscuro inherited from Straub and Jacques Tourneur. Cape Verdean immigrants from Lisbon's Fontainhas district perform versions of their own lives across decades of shooting. The framing is monumental, and it keeps the politics of that poverty in view.

    → Horse Money (2014) (list) · Casa de Lava (1994)

  10. Lisandro Alonso

    Argentina, b. 1975

    His films follow a single body moving through terrain with almost no dialogue, and the real duration of labour and travel is left intact. The later films break that observational patience with dream logic and anachronism, but the walk continues.

    → Los Muertos (2004) (seen) · La Libertad (2001) (list)

  11. Albert Serra

    Catalonia, b. 1975

    He leaves several cameras rolling for hours while non-actors improvise in historical costume, then carves the footage down in the edit. Boredom and decadence are his method. He applies it to the most canonical Western myths, from Quixote to Casanova and Louis XIV, as a kind of ritual deflation.

    → Honour of the Knights (2006) (list) · Liberté (2019) (seen)

  12. Angela Schanelec

    Germany, b. 1962

    Her films are elliptical to the edge of illegibility. The events that would organise a conventional film happen offscreen or are skipped. Feeling is flattened, framing is exact, and gesture follows Bresson, yet the emotion survives the removal of cause and effect.

    → Marseille (2004) (list) · Places in Cities (1998)

  13. Sohrab Shahid Saless

    Iran/Germany, 1944–1998

    His films repeat daily routine at something close to real time. There is almost no plot, and the camera doesn't comment on the poverty it records. He is a link between Bresson and Kiarostami and is still little discussed.

    → Still Life (1974) (list) · A Simple Event (1973) — Utopia (1983) is rated 4★ in the collection

  14. Luís Noronha da Costa

    Portugal, 1942–2020

    A painter and architect, he made about a dozen films in the 1970s and wrote criticism on Godard, Ozu, and Dreyer. The films carry his "anti-painting" of mirrors, frosted glass, and spray-diffused figures into gothic stories built from a handful of held shots.

    → O Construtor de Anjos (1978) (list) · D. Jaime ou a Noite Portuguesa (1974)

  15. Leonardo Favio

    Argentina, 1938–2012

    He began with stark black-and-white portraits of solitary boys and marginal men, close to Bresson in manner. He then became the most popular filmmaker in Argentina with operatic folk melodramas, so the same director stands at both ends of the national cinema.

    → Crónica de un niño solo (1965) · El dependiente (1969)

  16. Andrei Tarkovsky

    Soviet Union, 1932–1986

    He made seven features built from long takes that let time gather in the frame. Rain, fire, wind, and ruined rooms recur as material, and painters and poets recur as subjects. Where Bresson subtracts, Tarkovsky extends. He holds the shot until the world inside it changes.

    → Andrei Rublev (1966) (seen) · Mirror (1975) (seen)

  17. Víctor Erice

    Spain, b. 1940

    He has made four features in five decades, and each is a sealed room. A girl listens to Frankenstein, a father is remembered as a vanishing figure, and light moves across ripe fruit in a painter's studio. His films watch something until it changes.

    → The Spirit of the Beehive (1973) (seen) · Dream of Light (1992) (list)

  18. Ingmar Bergman

    Sweden, 1918–2007

    He applied the chamber drama to faith, illness, and marriage. Faces in close-up become landscapes, a theatre troupe's discipline shapes the work, and he spent four decades filming on Fårö and in the studio. Winter Light and Persona are the two ways in. One strips belief down to its ceremony and the other strips the self down to its image.

    → Sawdust and Tinsel (1953) · Summer Interlude (1951)

  19. Michael Haneke

    Austria, b. 1942

    He observes cruelty from a fixed distance. Bourgeois families come apart over what they refuse to look at, media spreads like a contagion, and every comfort the story seems to promise is held back. The rigour is moral as much as formal.

    → 71 Fragments of a Chronology of Chance (1994) (list) · After Liverpool (1974) (list)

  20. Béla Tarr

    Hungary, 1955–2026

    He moved from the social realism of the Béla Balázs Studio to the black-and-white long-take structures of Damnation, Sátántangó, and The Turin Horse. These films choreograph the movements of bodies, animals, and weather in mud and sodium light. He declared The Turin Horse his last film and kept his word.

    → Family Nest (1979) · The Prefab People (1982)

  21. Markus Schleinzer

    Austria, b. 1975

    A casting director turned director, he has made three films of near-silent control. Michael watches a locked room without comment, Angelo follows an African child brought to eighteenth-century Vienna, and Rose is the third. The cruelty lies in the distance, and the distance never wavers.

    → Michael (2011) (seen) · Angelo (2018) (seen)

  22. Darezhan Omirbayev

    Kazakhstan, b. 1958

    He is the quiet ironist of the Kazakh new wave. Provincial boys and mathematicians appear in long, patient frames, and Dostoevsky is reworked into post-Soviet Almaty. Kiarostami is the nearest neighbour, but the solitude is colder.

    → Kairat (1992) · The Road (2001)

Structural

  1. Jean-Marie Straub & Danièle Huillet

    France/Germany/Italy

    They always recorded direct sound and had texts delivered flat, so that language arrives as language. The camera is planted at the exact place where the historical violence happened. The wind, the traffic, and the cicadas in the take are subjects equal to the actor, and the films argue that the ground remembers.

    → Too Early, Too Late (1982) (list) · Sicilia! (1999)

  2. James Benning

    USA, b. 1942

    His films use a fixed camera, measured durations, and ambient sound. They treat landscape as the visible record of economic and political history. He turned looking into a discipline with a syntax you can count, which is why a film of his can be described in a sentence and still be unpredictable.

    → 13 Lakes (2004) · Ruhr (2009)

  3. Chantal Akerman

    Belgium, 1950–2015

    Her frontal, symmetrical framing lets a domestic gesture run at its real length, so that duration stops being incidental and holds the film up. Her places are hotel corridors, letters read over New York streets, and the apartment as both prison and world.

    → News from Home (1976) (seen) · From the East (1993) (list)

  4. Michael Snow

    Canada, 1928–2023

    Each of his films takes one mechanical operation and pursues it to exhaustion: a zoom in Wavelength, a pan in Back and Forth, a rotating arm in La Région centrale. Camera movement becomes the protagonist, and the viewer's shifting relationship to it is the drama.

    → La Région Centrale (1971) · Back and Forth (1969)

  5. Hollis Frampton

    USA, 1936–1984

    He made structural films organised by linguistic and mathematical systems. He came from photography and thought like a poet. His work is rigorous and also funny, which sets it apart in that tradition.

    → Zorns Lemma (1970) (list) · (nostalgia) (1971) (seen)

  6. Heinz Emigholz

    Germany, b. 1948

    He films buildings in hundreds of static shots with the camera deliberately tilted, and with no narration or music. The tilt is the argument. He refuses the "correct" architectural photograph and treats the camera as a body that leans instead of a neutral instrument.

    → Schindler's Houses (2007) · Parabeton (2012)

  7. Sharon Lockhart

    USA, b. 1964

    She brings a photographer's eye to a few very long takes of people working or performing, developed over long collaborations with her subjects. The duration reads as respect more than as rigour, and it brings a warmth that is unusual in the structural tradition.

    → Double Tide (2009) · Lunch Break (2008)

  8. Peter Hutton

    USA, 1944–2016

    His films are silent, fixed, and often black-and-white studies of rivers, ships, and weather. He had worked as a merchant seaman and framed like a nineteenth-century landscape painter. There is no narration, no music, and no argument, only the image.

    → At Sea (2007) (list) · Study of a River (1997)

  9. Peter Nestler

    Germany/Sweden, b. 1937

    He makes plain, quiet documentaries about mines, rivers, steelworks, and minority communities, with no interest in style for its own sake. Straub called him the most important German documentarian. The films are short and unglamorous, and their moral position is clear.

    → Mülheim (Ruhr) (1964) · The Northern Calotte (1991)

  10. Jean-Claude Rousseau

    France, b. 1946

    He makes films alone with a small camera in hotel rooms and apartments, and holds each frame far past comfort. It is Straub's materialism turned inward and domestic. Solitude is given a structure instead of a subject.

    → Les Antiquités de Rome (1989) · La Vallée close (1995) (list)

  11. Harun Farocki

    Germany, 1944–2014

    He analysed found and operational images on screen, often comparing two channels side by side. His sources include factory surveillance, weapons guidance, and training simulations. The films think with images instead of about them.

    → Inextinguishable Fire (1969) (seen) · Respite (2007) (seen)

  12. Chris Marker

    France, 1921–2012

    He shaped the essay film: voice-over as its own tense, memory as an unreliable narrator, travel footage, cats, and a lightly worn erudition. He made his own subjectivity into a documentary method.

    → A Grin Without a Cat (1977) · Level Five (1997) (list)

  13. Adam Curtis

    UK, b. 1955

    He cuts BBC archive footage into dense montage under deadpan narration and pop music, tracing sweeping stories of cause and effect about power and psychology. The arguments are contested as history, and the method is collage.

    → Bitter Lake (2015) · Can't Get You Out of My Head (2021)

  14. Masao Adachi

    Japan, b. 1939

    He co-founded the landscape theory known as fūkeiron. It holds that the state's power is visible in the landscape itself, so the film shows only landscape. He then left filmmaking to join the Japanese Red Army in Lebanon, which makes the theory hard to separate from the life.

    → A.K.A. Serial Killer (1969) (list) · Prisoner/Terrorist (2007)

  15. Shinsuke Ogawa

    Japan, 1935–1992

    He made documentary a matter of living together for years. His collective moved in with the farmers resisting Narita airport, and later moved to a village and learned to grow rice before filming it. Duration is measured in years instead of minutes.

    → Sanrizuka: Heta Village (1973) (list) · Magino Village — A Tale (1986)

  16. Bill Morrison

    USA, b. 1965

    He sets decayed nitrate footage to contemporary scores, so the physical destruction of the image becomes the content. His subject is film itself as it decays.

    → Decasia (2002) (list) · Dawson City: Frozen Time (2016)

  17. Kamal Aljafari

    Palestine, b. 1972

    He digitally erases the actors from Israeli commercial films shot in Jaffa and leaves the Palestinians who happened to be in frame. He also works directly on looted archives. The method is restoration and critique at once.

    → Recollection (2015) · A Fidai Film (2024)

  18. Trinh T. Minh-ha

    Vietnam/USA, b. 1952

    She refuses ethnographic authority as a formal principle, which she calls "speaking nearby". Her films use disjunctive sound, repetition, and theory spoken as voice-over. They act out the argument instead of illustrating it.

    → Reassemblage (1982) · Naked Spaces — Living Is Round (1985)

  19. Gregory J. Markopoulos

    USA/Greece, 1928–1992

    He made films of rapid single-frame montage and mythic, classical portraiture. He then withdrew his whole body of work from circulation so that it could be screened only in a field in Arcadia. The conditions of screening became part of the work.

    → Twice a Man (1963) (list) · Ming Green (1966)

  20. Robert Beavers

    USA, b. 1949

    His editing is precise and musical, with anamorphic masking and a steady reflexivity: his own hand, the camera, and the act of filming folded into the image. Nearly all of his work belongs to one cycle that he has spent fifty years making and revising.

    → From the Notebook of… (1971/98) (list) · The Hedge Theater (1986–2002)

Nocturnal

  1. Philippe Grandrieux

    France, b. 1954

    His camera is handheld and often out of focus, with bodies half-dissolved into darkness and sound designed as physical pressure instead of information. The films address the nervous system before the mind. Narrative is present but works like a rumour.

    → White Epilepsy (2012) · Malgré la nuit (2015)

  2. Scott Barley

    Wales, b. 1991

    He pushes digital darkness to near-total black and layers phone footage until a landscape becomes a cosmic event, with the human figure almost entirely gone. Sleep Has Her House was made alone with a phone and a laptop.

    → In the Mouth a Heart (2015) · the Vimeo shorts — Sleep Has Her House is #87 on the favourites list

  3. Fred Kelemen

    Germany, b. 1964

    His films move through wet cities at night in very long handheld takes, with colour bleeding toward black and despair treated as a kind of weather. He later became Béla Tarr's cinematographer, but in his own films the night is the subject instead of the setting.

    → Frost (1998) · Fallen (2005) (list)

  4. James Fotopoulos

    USA, b. 1976

    He works in grainy black and white with static frames and tiny budgets, and he has made well over a hundred works. Bodies are filmed with a clinical coldness until horror appears without any of the machinery of genre.

    → Zero (1997) · Migrating Forms (1999) — Back Against the Wall is #63 on the favourites list

  5. Lois Patiño

    Galicia, b. 1983

    His human figures stand frozen like statues in vast landscapes while the natural world keeps moving. Whispered voices sound close while the bodies stay far away, and saturated single-colour tinting works as an omen. He turns the tableau around, so the land acts and the people don't.

    → Coast of Death (2013) (list) · Montaña en sombra (2012)

  6. Mauro Herce

    Spain, b. 1976

    His portraits are frontal, holding a still human figure inside a moving element such as water, a ship, or light treated as something with weight. His style is recognisable in the films he shoots for other directors, so it forms a line of its own.

    → Ocaso (2010) (list) · as cinematographer: Longa noite, Mimosas, Aqueronte

  7. Šarūnas Bartas

    Lithuania, b. 1964

    He holds faces for minutes with almost no dialogue, in the ruined edges of the post-Soviet world. He removes speech almost entirely, yet the films stay physical instead of turning abstract.

    → Few of Us (1996) · The Corridor (1995) (seen)

  8. Rouzbeh Rashidi

    Iran/Ireland, b. 1980

    He is very prolific and makes hybrid work that layers analogue and digital footage, with the atmosphere of horror but no horror plot, and narrative unmoored on purpose. He founded the Experimental Film Society and built its own way of making and distributing the films.

    → Phantom Islands (2018) (list) · He (2013)

  9. Dean Kavanagh

    Ireland, b. 1990

    He has made around seventy shorts and six features that combine materialist film methods with old and new formats, circling time, biology, and landscape. Unlike most filmmakers in this corner, he keeps narrative structure in play.

    → Polar Nights (2015) · Friends with Johnny Kline (2016)

  10. Kyle Faulkner

    New Zealand

    He works entirely alone, with one camera across six countries and a decade between shooting and finishing. He uses landscape and slow-cinema duration for something essayistic and openly personal, and he publishes the work free online.

    → Hitokara (2018) · ________ (2018) (seen)

  11. Eloy Enciso

    Galicia, b. 1975

    His non-actors read letters and testimony from the Franco years, with faces emerging from near-total darkness and the delivery kept deliberately flat. It is the Straubs' method applied to one silenced history, and it brings out what the Galician landscape films leave unsaid.

    → Endless Night / Longa noite (2019) (seen) · Arraianos (2012)

  12. Emilija Škarnulytė

    Lithuania, b. 1987

    A mythic figure, often the artist herself as a mermaid, drifts through decommissioned nuclear reactors, Arctic military sites, and flooded infrastructure. The work is made for galleries but runs at the length of cinema, and its real subject is geological time.

    → Sirenomelia (2017) · Burial (2022) (seen)

  13. Oliver Laxe

    France/Galicia, b. 1982

    Born in France and based in Galicia, he makes fictions that erase their own maker. You Are All Captains questions the filmmaker's role, Mimosas is a mystical road film, Fire Will Come burns through eucalyptus, and Sirāt searches through all-night raves. The sacred appears as sound, smoke, and crowd.

    → You Are All Captains (2010) · Mimosas (2016) (list)

Modernist

  1. Michelangelo Antonioni

    Italy, 1912–2007

    He held architecture and empty space in the frame after the characters had left, let the narrative dissolve into landscape, and designed colour as a state of mind. Much of the language of alienation used in this guide comes from his films.

    → Il Grido (1957) · Identification of a Woman (1982)

  2. Hong Sang-soo

    South Korea, b. 1960

    His films use static tables, sudden zooms, soju, and embarrassment, and they are built on repetition with variation: the same day or the same encounter, told differently. The method stays so consistent across more than thirty films that the filmography reads as one long serial novel.

    → On the Beach at Night Alone (2017) · A Traveler's Needs (2024)

  3. Tsai Ming-liang

    Taiwan/Malaysia, b. 1957

    His films use static long takes, water leaking everywhere, decaying buildings, and near-total silence. Lee Kang-sheng's body ages across thirty years of them. The slowness is an endurance test for the actor and the viewer at once.

    → Stray Dogs (2013) · then the Walker cycle

  4. Abbas Kiarostami

    Iran, 1940–2016

    His films are made of cars, roads, children, and films within films. He probed the boundary between documentary and fiction until it stopped being a boundary, and he turned ethical self-questioning into the form of the film.

    → Five (2003) (seen) · The Traveler (1974)

  5. Éric Rohmer

    France, 1920–2010

    His films are made of talk, seasons, and natural light. His characters build elaborate justifications for what they were always going to do anyway, and the conversation carries the film without ever feeling like theatre.

    → A Tale of Winter (1992) · The Marquise of O (1976) (list)

  6. Kira Muratova

    Ukraine, 1934–2018

    She used repetition, shouting, non-sequitur, grotesque casting, and sudden tenderness, all within tightly controlled frames. Her films are difficult in texture more than in length, and the abrasion works as a moral instrument.

    → Brief Encounters (1967) · The Asthenic Syndrome (1989)

  7. Otar Iosseliani

    Georgia/France, 1934–2023

    His films have no protagonist and no plot engine. Ensembles drift through villages and cities with a dry comic timing, and the warmth is anarchic while the composition is exact. The difficulty is in the slackness, not in any obscurity.

    → There Once Was a Singing Blackbird (1970) (list) · Pastorale (1975)

  8. Luis Buñuel

    Spain/Mexico/France, 1900–1983

    He was a surrealist first and a filmmaker across five decades and three countries. The dream logic of Un Chien Andalou hardens in the Mexican period into cool social satire. Reality never settles: a dinner party cannot leave the room, a beggar may be a saint, and the camera watches both with the same appetite.

    → Susana (1951) · Robinson Crusoe (1954)

  9. Jean-Luc Godard

    France/Switzerland, 1930–2022

    He spent sixty years arguing with cinema from inside it, from the jump cuts of the New Wave and the dialectics of the Dziga Vertov years to the late video essays that fold image, text, and sound into one long sentence. Each period is a different filmmaker, and what continues is the questioning.

    → Here and Elsewhere (1976) (list) · Film Socialisme (2010) (list)

  10. Claire Denis

    France, b. 1948

    Her films put bodies before plots. Colonial childhoods, legionnaires' routines, and families at kitchen tables are filmed so close that touch carries the story. She treats genre as material to loosen, from horror to science fiction.

    → U.S. Go Home (1994) (list) · Both Sides of the Blade (2022) (list)

  11. Rainer Werner Fassbinder

    West Germany, 1945–1982

    He made more than forty films in fifteen years. He used melodrama as a social X-ray of West Germany, its kitchens, offices, and marriage beds, filmed with Douglas Sirk's grammar turned colder. The speed of the output is itself part of the work.

    → Mother Küsters Goes to Heaven (1975) (list) · I Only Want You to Love Me (1976) (list)

  12. Pier Paolo Pasolini

    Italy, 1922–1975

    He was a poet, a novelist, and a dissident Marxist. His films move from the margins of the Roman outskirts (Accattone, Mamma Roma) through myth retold in flesh (The Gospel, the Trilogy of Life) to the allegory of Salò. They are sacred and scatological at once, with the sacred rendered through bodies.

    → Oedipus Rex (1967) · Medea (1969)

  13. Hiroshi Teshigahara

    Japan, 1927–2001

    His father founded the Sōgetsu school of flower arrangement, which Hiroshi later ran. He made four features with the novelist Kobo Abe and the composer Toru Takemitsu, treating identity as matter and matter as a trap, shot with a documentarist's patience. After The Ruined Map (1968) he made only a few more films and turned largely to ceramics, bamboo, and the school.

    → The Ruined Map (1968) · Summer Soldiers (1972)

  14. Jean Eustache

    France, 1938–1981

    His small body of work has two poles. The Mother and the Whore is a post-1968 marathon of talk, and Numéro zéro holds one fixed camera on his grandmother as she remembers. He made fewer films than the other heirs of the New Wave, and he is one of the most argued over.

    → The Pig (1970) (list) · Numéro zéro (2003)

  15. John Cassavetes

    USA, 1929–1989

    He was the actor-director at the start of American independent cinema. He self-financed films on 16mm, loosened his scripts for improvisation, and used the long close-up as an instrument. Faces, A Woman Under the Influence, and Opening Night put marriage and performance through the wringer together.

    → Minnie and Moskowitz (1971) · Love Streams (1984) (list)

  16. Radu Jude

    Romania, b. 1977

    He is the ironist of the Romanian new wave. His deadpan, fixed-camera realism is loosened by archive footage, title cards, and internet debris until essay and fiction collapse into each other. His subject is how images and shame circulate in post-communist Romania.

    → The Dead Nation (2017) · The Diary of a Chambermaid (2026) (list)

Tricontinental

  1. Lav Diaz

    Philippines, b. 1958

    His films are black and white, static, and very long. Philippine colonial history and the Marcos dictatorship are given the running time it would take to live through them. The duration is an argument about historical time more than a choice of style.

    → Melancholia (2008) · From What Is Before (2014) (list)

  2. Miguel Gomes

    Portugal, b. 1972

    He shifts genre and register within a single film, turning fable into documentary into silent-film pastiche, and melancholy irony holds it together. The structural games leave room for warmth.

    → Our Beloved Month of August (2008) · Redemption (2013)

  3. Lucrecia Martel

    Argentina, b. 1966

    Sound design is her main narrative instrument. Bodies are cropped half out of frame, and a humid stillness and class dread build up without any inciting incident. She creates unease through the ear, which is why her films are so hard to describe visually.

    → The Headless Woman (2008) · The Holy Girl (2004)

  4. Mani Kaul

    India, 1944–2011

    He was Ghatak's student and worked from the sense of time in Indian classical music instead of Western drama. His films elaborate instead of developing, and they explore space by moving the camera instead of cutting. The delivery has Bresson's flatness, with the structure of a raga underneath.

    → Dhrupad (1983) · Siddheshwari (1989)

  5. Ritwik Ghatak

    India/Bangladesh, 1925–1976

    He pushed melodrama to an expressionist extreme, with wide-angle distortion and sound used as an assault, and the trauma of Partition as his permanent subject. The emotion is at its fullest while the form stays strict, which makes him the opposite pole from Ray.

    → The Cloud-Capped Star (1960) (list) · Subarnarekha (1965) (list)

  6. Djibril Diop Mambéty

    Senegal, 1945–1998

    His films use jump cuts and sound collage, and African myth collides with colonial modernity in them. There is a wildness that stays funny. He was an avant-gardist working in a popular register.

    → Touki Bouki (1973) (list) · Hyenas (1992) (list)

  7. Tariq Teguia

    Algeria, b. 1966

    His films are built on wandering. Wide anamorphic desert compositions follow militants, surveyors, and border crossings, and the landscape is treated as a space of relations instead of an emptiness. The films draw openly on Édouard Glissant and are composed like geometry.

    → Inland / Gabbla (2008) (list) · Rome Rather Than You (2006)

  8. Med Hondo

    Mauritania/France, 1935–2019

    He made furious, formally anarchic essay-fictions about African immigrant life and colonial history, which turn into theatre or musical without warning. It is a diasporic modernism with no interest in respectability.

    → Soleil Ô (1970) (list) · West Indies (1979)

  9. Safi Faye

    Senegal, 1943–2023

    An ethnographer, she turned the discipline inside out by filming her own Serer village with the villagers as collaborators, at the pace of farming life. She was the first sub-Saharan African woman to direct a commercially distributed feature.

    → Kaddu Beykat (1975) · Mossane (1996)

  10. Andrea Tonacci

    Brazil, 1944–2016

    He began with aggressive, fractured slapstick in the years of Brazil's Cinema Marginal. He then spent decades on a film in which an Indigenous man re-enacts his own displacement. Documentary and re-enactment collapse into each other, handled with great ethical care.

    → Serras da Desordem (2006) · Bang Bang (1971)

  11. Ousmane Sembène

    Senegal, 1923–2007

    A novelist, he turned to cinema in his forties, arguing that film reached the audience his books could not. Black Girl was the first feature by a sub-Saharan African director to reach an international audience. His later films moved to Wolof and to a broader social address.

    → Black Girl (1966) (seen) · Mandabi (1968)

  12. Roberto Gavaldón

    Mexico, 1909–1986

    He was the dark classicist of Mexico's golden age, making gothic melodramas and noirs of great precision, with Gabriel Figueroa's chiaroscuro as co-author. Buñuel's Mexican years are the obvious neighbour.

    → El Rebozo de Soledad (1952) · La Escondida (1956)

  13. Apichatpong Weerasethakul

    Thailand, b. 1970

    He films jungle medicine and reincarnation with an unbroken calm. His films are often in two parts, where myth and daytime realism share the same bed, and light and sound are treated as physical presences. Memoria (2021) was released as a travelling object, shown in one cinema at a time.

    → Syndromes and a Century (2006) (list) · A Letter to Uncle Boonmee (2009)
