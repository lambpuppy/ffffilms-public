    • affinity Dimitri Kirsanoff Kirsanoff's Ménilmontant (1926) takes the rapid cutting and superimposition of Epstein's Coeur fidèle (1923) to a working-class Paris melodrama.

1940s–50s

  1. Robert Bresson

    Diary of a Country Priest (1951) · favourites #14 · favourite tier 1 · 11 seen · 6 at 4.5+ · Spiritual minimalism

    • affinity Jean-Pierre Dardenne & Luc Dardenne The Dardennes and Bresson carry moral gravity in a body in motion. Rosetta (1999) pursues Mouchette (1967) with a handheld camera.
    • affinity Bruno Dumont Dumont and Bresson hold non-actors in frames that wait for grace. Humanité (1999) borrows Bresson's discipline for a different metaphysics.
    • affinity Angela Schanelec Schanelec and Bresson keep the decisive event offscreen and let ellipsis produce the feeling. Music (2023) withholds its catastrophe the way Bresson withheld his.
    • affinity Cyril Schäublin Schäublin's Unrest (2022) casts non-professionals and films hands at work on watch parts. Cinema Scope finds in it Bresson's transactional economy of gesture.
    • affinity Carl Theodor Dreyer Dreyer and Bresson film the face as a spiritual landscape. Both strip cinema to the face, the fact, and the footstep.
    • affinity Mani Kaul In Duvidha (1973) Kaul took Bresson's stripped non-actor delivery into a Rajasthan folk tale.
    • affinity Alain Cavalier Cavalier's Thérèse films rooms, hands, and seasons, and records the interior life as an inventory. It is the canonical Bressonian biopic.

  2. Yasujirō Ozu

    Floating Weeds (1959) · favourites #29 · favourite tier 3 · 16 seen · 12 at 4.5+ · Japanese humanists

    • affinity Hong Sang-soo Ozu and Hong return to meals, rooms, and repetition. Both play the same scene again with the feeling changed.
    • affinity Hou Hsiao-hsien Ozu and Hou share the fixed camera, domestic interiors, and ellipsis. Hou made Café Lumière (2003) for the Ozu centenary.
    • affinity Aki Kaurismäki Kaurismäki's La Vie de Bohème (1992) has a static kindness. It carries Ozu's patience with losers over to three penniless artists in Paris.
    • affinity Mikio Naruse Naruse and Ozu share the same tatami-height geometry and ellipsis. Ozu's ellipsis consoles, while Naruse's grinds. When a Woman Ascends the Stairs runs the grammar to harder ends.
    • affinity Sohrab Shahid-Saless Still Life's static domestic waiting transposes Ozu's grammar to Tehran. It belongs to the Iranian minimalism Saless started.
    • affinity Wim Wenders In Tokyo-Ga Wenders filmed his pilgrimage to Ozu's Tokyo and found the reverence for the ordinary unchanged. The film is the argument.

  3. John Cassavetes

    Shadows (1958) · favourites #128 · favourite tier 2 · 7 seen · 1 at 4.5+ · Location realism

    • affinity Maurice Pialat Cassavetes and Pialat were actor-directors who shot marriage as attrition. The house in A Woman Under the Influence and Pialat's kitchens both run without relief.
    • affinity Mike Leigh Leigh's workshop-built families and Cassavetes's ragged ensembles study performance until the seams show. In both the rehearsal room is the film.
    • affinity Claudia Weill Weill made Girlfriends the Cassavetes way, with documentary training, her own money, New York apartments, and three years of shooting. The friendship is the whole weather of the plot.
    • affinity Vincent Gallo Buffalo '66 has a flinching intimacy. It aims Cassavetes's unpolished emotional close-ups at one wounded man.
    • affinity Josh Safdie Uncut Gems puts performance in a pressure cooker and plays New York as a nervous system. It runs on Cassavetes fuel.

  4. Douglas Sirk

    All That Heaven Allows (1955) · added by hand · favourite tier 4 · 3 seen · 2 at 4.5+ · Melodrama

    • affinity Rainer Werner Fassbinder Sirk and Fassbinder play melodrama straight as a social X-ray. Fassbinder remade All That Heaven Allows (1955) as Ali: Fear Eats the Soul (1974).
    • affinity Pedro Almodóvar Sirk and Almodóvar style women's suffering to the limit until the style becomes the argument. All About My Mother (1999) runs Sirk's method in saturated colour.
    • affinity Alfred Hitchcock Hitchcock and Sirk smuggle classical Hollywood irony through décor. The meaning sits in the mise-en-scène (a window, a staircase) while the plot pretends to be the point.

  6. Maya Deren

    • affinity Gregory J. Markopoulos Deren and Markopoulos made myth and ritual into film form. They belong to the American avant-garde that traded narrative for rite.

  7. Carl Theodor Dreyer

  8. Jean-Pierre Melville

1960s–70s

  1. Luis Buñuel

    Belle de Jour (1967) · favourites #4 · favourite tier 1 · 19 seen · 14 at 4.5+ · Deadpan absurd

    • affinity Pedro Almodóvar Buñuel and Almodóvar treat desire as blasphemy against Catholic decorum and deliver it deadpan. The Spanish line runs from Viridiana (1961) to Bad Education (2004).
    • affinity Man Ray Charles de Noailles produced Man Ray's Les Mystères du Château de Dé (1929) and Buñuel's L'Âge d'or (1930). They are Surrealist films made for one patron.
    • affinity Anselmo Duarte Duarte's O Pagador de Promessas (1962) and Buñuel's Nazarín (1959) each follow a literal-minded believer. In both the Church his devotion was meant for refuses it.
    • affinity Luis García Berlanga Berlanga's Plácido and Buñuel's Viridiana (both 1961) turn Catholic charity towards the poor into a staged ritual and let it curdle.
    • affinity Pere Portabella Portabella produced Buñuel's Viridiana and spent his career unbottling what Buñuel smuggled past Franco. His Vampir-Cuadecuc is the making-of as ghost story.
    • affinity Ainhoa Rodríguez Rodríguez's Mighty Flash (2021) returns to Extremadura, the ground of Buñuel's Las Hurdes (1933). Both have villagers play themselves and tip rural rite into the surreal.
