  1. Robert Bresson

    Diary of a Country Priest (1951) · favourite tier 1 · 11 seen · Transcendental · Spiritual minimalism

    • affinity Angela Schanelec Schanelec and Bresson keep the decisive event offscreen and let ellipsis produce the feeling. Music (2023) withholds its catastrophe the way Bresson withheld his.
    • affinity Mani Kaul In Duvidha (1973) Kaul took Bresson's stripped non-actor delivery into a Rajasthan folk tale.
    • affinity Jean-Pierre Dardenne & Luc Dardenne The Dardennes and Bresson carry moral gravity in a body in motion. Rosetta (1999) pursues Mouchette (1967) with a handheld camera.
    • affinity Bruno Dumont Dumont and Bresson hold non-actors in frames that wait for grace. Humanité (1999) borrows Bresson's discipline for a different metaphysics.
    • affinity Carl Theodor Dreyer Dreyer and Bresson film the face as a spiritual landscape. Both strip cinema to the face, the fact, and the footstep.
    • affinity Alain Cavalier Cavalier's Thérèse films rooms, hands, and seasons, and records the interior life as an inventory. It is the canonical Bressonian biopic.
    • influenced Paul Schrader Bresson ends Pickpocket (1959) with a prison visit. Schrader restages it in American Gigolo (1980) and Light Sleeper (1992).
    • influenced Michael Haneke Haneke wrote an essay on Bresson's Au hasard Balthazar (1966) and put two Bresson films first in his Sight and Sound list.
    • influenced Aki Kaurismäki Kaurismäki's The Match Factory Girl (1990) follows Bresson's Mouchette (1967). Affectless acting and ellipsis run through Kaurismäki's films.
    • influenced Hong Sang-soo Hong calls seeing Bresson's Diary of a Country Priest (1951) at the Art Institute of Chicago 'a turning point'.
    • influenced Jean-Pierre Dardenne & Luc Dardenne The Dardennes work with non-professional actors, no score, and no psychology. Their long-stated admiration for Bresson shows in the method.
    • influenced Mani Kaul Kaul's Uski Roti (1970) takes Bresson's delayed cuts, flat line readings, and close-ups of hands.
    • influenced Harun Farocki Farocki named Bresson and Straub-Huillet as the filmmakers who influenced him.
    • influenced Angela Schanelec Seeing Bresson's L'Argent (1983) made Schanelec stop acting. She says his language is impossible to use without thinking of him.
    • influenced Darezhan Omirbayev From Kairat (1992) to Student (2012) Omirbayev films even professional actors as ordinary people. His realism follows Bresson's austere method.
    • influenced Louis Malle Malle assisted Bresson on A Man Escaped (1956). His Lacombe, Lucien (1974) casts a non-professional by his face, as Bresson did.
    • influenced Andrei Tarkovsky Bresson's Diary of a Country Priest (1951) heads Tarkovsky's 1972 list of ten favourite films.
    • influenced Bertrand Bonello Bonello says he was 'very obsessed by Robert Bresson' when he began making films.
    • worked with Jean Cocteau Cocteau wrote the dialogue for Bresson's Les Dames du Bois de Boulogne (1945).
    • worked with Louis Malle Malle was Bresson's assistant on A Man Escaped (1956) and was in charge of the escape props.
    • worked with Jean-Marie Straub & Danièle Huillet Straub worked as Bresson's assistant in Paris in the mid-1950s.
    • influenced Oliver Laxe After moving to Morocco Laxe settled on three masters. He names Bresson "the mind" of them (Film Comment, on Sirât, 2025).
    • influenced Kelly Reichardt Reichardt teaches Bresson and Ozu as models of filmmakers who "use the formal language of the frame and try to slow things down" (2023).
    • affinity Cyril Schäublin Schäublin's Unrest (2022) casts non-professionals and films hands at work on watch parts. Cinema Scope finds in it Bresson's transactional economy of gesture.
    • affinity Rouzbeh Rashidi Rashidi holds non-actors flat inside fragmented, elliptical films such as He (2012). The critic Bill Mousoulis called him a 21st-century Bresson.
    • influenced Leonardo Favio Favio cuts Polín's escape in Crónica de un niño solo (1965) on hands, objects, and held silence. That is the manner of Bresson's A Man Escaped (1956).
    • influenced Jean-Claude Rousseau Rousseau met Bresson and works from Bresson's Notes. He takes from them the rule that the image must end as a flat surface, 'ironed like a shirt'.

    • influenced Hal Hartley Filmmaker magazine framed Godard as Hartley's mentor for their 1994 conversation. Hartley credits Godard's 1980s video work with his economy.
    • worked with Jean-Pierre Melville Melville plays the novelist Parvulesco interviewed at Orly in Godard's Breathless (1960).
    • worked with Éric Rohmer Godard acted in Rohmer's Charlotte et son steak (1951) and directed Rohmer's script All the Boys Are Called Patrick (1957).

    • worked with Chris Marker Marker initiated and edited Far from Vietnam (1967), which includes Godard's segment.
    • worked with Jean Eustache Godard gave Eustache leftover Masculin Féminin stock and is executive producer of Eustache's Le Père Noël a les yeux bleus (1966).
    • influenced David Lynch Lynch named Godard among his three heroes (The Talks, 2014). He also insisted "it's not about copying".
    • affinity Kamal Aljafari Godard's Ici et ailleurs (1976) and Aljafari's A Fidai Film (2024) both return to Palestinian footage after the fact. They ask what montage can still do.
    • affinity Masao Adachi Adachi made Red Army/PFLP (1971), and Godard's abandoned Jusqu'à la victoire later became Ici et ailleurs (1976). Both directors went to the fedayeen in 1970–71 with militant cameras.
    • affinity Heinz Emigholz Emigholz's 2+2=22 [The Alphabet] (2017) films Kreidler recording in Tbilisi. Hyperallergic (2017) calls it Emigholz's version of Godard's One Plus One (1968).

    • affinity Masao Adachi Adachi's A.K.A. Serial Killer (1969) and Straub-Huillet's Fortini/Cani (1976) both film landscapes as the site where political violence left its trace.

  4. Luis Buñuel

    Belle de Jour (1967) · favourite tier 1 · 19 seen · Modernist (also Nocturnal) · Deadpan absurd

    • affinity Pedro Almodóvar Buñuel and Almodóvar treat desire as blasphemy against Catholic decorum and deliver it deadpan. The Spanish line runs from Viridiana (1961) to Bad Education (2004).
    • affinity Roberto Gavaldón Gavaldón filmed Mexican fatalism in black and white and treated poverty and the supernatural as plain fact. His films stand beside Buñuel's own Mexican period.
    • affinity Yorgos Lanthimos Buñuel's The Exterminating Angel seals a dinner and Lanthimos's Dogtooth seals a house. Both push bourgeois protocol until it curdles into deadpan horror.
    • affinity Pere Portabella Portabella produced Buñuel's Viridiana and spent his career unbottling what Buñuel smuggled past Franco. His Vampir-Cuadecuc is the making-of as ghost story.
    • worked with Jean Epstein Buñuel was Epstein's assistant director on Mauprat (1926) and on The Fall of the House of Usher (1928), which he left.
    • worked with Pere Portabella Portabella's company Films 59 co-produced Viridiana (1961) and brought Buñuel back to shoot in Spain.
    • affinity Luis García Berlanga Berlanga's Plácido and Buñuel's Viridiana (both 1961) turn Catholic charity towards the poor into a staged ritual and let it curdle.
    • affinity Man Ray Charles de Noailles produced Man Ray's Les Mystères du Château de Dé (1929) and Buñuel's L'Âge d'or (1930). They are Surrealist films made for one patron.
    • affinity Ainhoa Rodríguez Rodríguez's Mighty Flash (2021) returns to Extremadura, the ground of Buñuel's Las Hurdes (1933). Both have villagers play themselves and tip rural rite into the surreal.
    • affinity Anselmo Duarte Duarte's O Pagador de Promessas (1962) and Buñuel's Nazarín (1959) each follow a literal-minded believer. In both the Church his devotion was meant for refuses it.
    • affinity João César Monteiro Monteiro's God's Comedy (1995) and Buñuel's The Milky Way (1969) are anticlerical erotic comedies. Both play desire and blasphemy deadpan through a man of fixed habits.
    • affinity Marco Bellocchio Bellocchio's Fists in the Pocket (1965) turns Buñuel's bourgeois family rot into matricide and plays it as black comedy.
    • influenced Rouzbeh Rashidi Rashidi's own synopsis calls Ten Years in the Sun (2015) a tribute to the subversive wit of Buñuel.
    • affinity Otar Iosseliani In Iosseliani's French films bourgeois life is a circular farce. His Favourites of the Moon (1984) passes objects between owners as Buñuel's The Discreet Charm (1972) passes dinners.

    • affinity Víctor Erice Tarkovsky's Mirror (1975) and Erice's The Spirit of the Beehive (1973) were made two years apart. Both film childhood memory as atmosphere and the interior life as landscape.

    • influenced Phạm Thiên Ân Phạm's Inside the Yellow Cocoon Shell (2023) moves by weather, water, and long takes. He names Tarkovsky among five directors who influenced him.
    • affinity František Vláčil Vláčil's Marketa Lazarová (1967) and Tarkovsky's Andrei Rublev (1966) are parallel state-funded medieval epics in black and white. Vláčil's camera is restless where Tarkovsky's waits.

  8. Abbas Kiarostami

    Taste of Cherry (1997) · favourite tier 2 · 23 seen · Modernist (also Tricontinental) · Iranian new wave & kin

    • affinity Mohsen Makhmalbaf Kiarostami and Makhmalbaf deliberately blur fiction and documentary with non-professional actors. Close-Up (1990) stages the question they share, which is where the man ends and the performance begins.
    • affinity Darezhan Omirbayev Kiarostami and Omirbayev make intimate, minimal films in small towns, with deadpan ellipsis, non-actors, and roads. Omirbayev's Kazakh films run on Kiarostami's grammar.
    • affinity Joshua Oppenheimer Kiarostami and Oppenheimer re-stage reality so that it reveals itself. The courtroom reenactments of Close-Up anticipate the restaged massacres of The Act of Killing (2012).
    • affinity Satyajit Ray Ray and Kiarostami share a rural humanism with non-professional actors and use the village road as the narrative line. Ray's example was watched closely in Iran.
    • affinity Forugh Farrokhzad The lepers of The House Is Black and the boy of Where Is the Friend's House share Iranian cinema's ethical gaze at the humble. Farrokhzad wrote its grammar first.
    • affinity Ryusuke Hamaguchi Drive My Car's rolling two-shots rehearse Kiarostami's dashboard confessions. For both directors the vehicle is the room where truth is told.
    • worked with Mohsen Makhmalbaf Sabzian impersonated Makhmalbaf, who appears as himself in the final scene of Kiarostami's Close-Up (1990).
    • influenced by Yasujirō Ozu Kiarostami's Five Dedicated to Ozu (2003) is made of five fixed-camera takes that never move, zoom, or pan. He dedicated it to Ozu.
    • influenced by Forugh Farrokhzad Kiarostami took the title of The Wind Will Carry Us (1999) from a Farrokhzad poem. His debt is to Farrokhzad the poet more than to the filmmaker.
    • influenced Oliver Laxe When discussing Sirât (2025) Laxe cites Kiarostami's Taste of Cherry (1997) on death. He calls Kiarostami "the heart" of his three masters.
    • influenced Apichatpong Weerasethakul Apichatpong says Kiarostami "was very important" among the films he kept watching (Pajarera del Medio interview, 2017).
    • affinity Andrea Tonacci In Tonacci's Serras da Desordem (2006) and Kiarostami's Close-Up (1990) the real man re-enacts his own story for the camera. Both films show the staging.

    • affinity Miklós Jancsó Jancsó's The Round-Up (1966) and Tarr's Sátántangó (1994) are both set on the Hungarian plains. Both make the unbroken take the unit of a film.

  12. Yasujirō Ozu

    • affinity Hong Sang-soo Ozu and Hong return to meals, rooms, and repetition. Both play the same scene again with the feeling changed.
